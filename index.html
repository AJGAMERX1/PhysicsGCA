Guidelines (not a checklist)

Not every day is a new-material day — some days are review, some are catch-up, some are just problems. That's fine; write down whatever today actually is.

When it is new material, try to encode it before you retrieve anything else: read it, work the examples, explain each step in your own words — don't just re-read. If you can, leave a retrieval pass for later in the day rather than right after.

Whatever the day looks like, a couple of honest lines below is enough to make you commit to it.

why this works

Hermann Ebbinghaus showed over a century ago that most new material decays fast — often 50–70% is gone within a day if it's never revisited. The review ladder (1 → 3 → 7 → 16 → 35 days) exists because Cepeda et al.'s meta-analyses found that gap should expand over time, not stay fixed.

Roediger & Karpicke found that actively pulling information out of memory beats re-reading, even though re-reading feels more productive — that's why the guidelines push retrieval, not review, and why coursework is something you complete, not just reread.

Rohrer & Taylor found mixing topics (interleaving) lowers same-day quiz scores but roughly doubles next-day retention, because it forces you to identify which method applies. That's why "tonight's pick" mixes modules instead of drilling one.

Walker & Stickgold's sleep research shows the brain stabilizes recent learning during sleep, especially the first night — which is why a retrieval pass late in the day is worth more than one right after learning.

Justin Sung's framework (priming → encoding → retrieval → overlearning) argues most students jump to rereading/rewatching before material is properly encoded. Flowmodoro — work until focus breaks, then rest for elapsed time ÷ ~5 — is his fix for rigid Pomodoro timing.
